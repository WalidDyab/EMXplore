Radio Astronomy · Prince Sultan University

Turning historical radio observations into new science

Several generations of telescopes at PSU and PNU have been listening to neutral hydrogen in the Milky Way. We are reprocessing those observations into traceable, reproducible datasets that students can keep analysing.

What the project is

Cold hydrogen gas in the Galaxy emits a faint radio line at 1420.40575 MHz, a wavelength of about 21 cm. It passes through the dust that hides much of the Galaxy from optical telescopes, and its Doppler shift tells us how fast the gas moves.

The EMXplore Radio Astronomy project is the research and learning environment for these observations. It does not only document the telescopes and their history. It turns the observations accumulated over several generations of instruments into datasets that are traceable, reproducible and scientifically useful.

The telescopes

  • PSU fixed telescope (historical): a 5 m reflector pointing straight up. The Earth's rotation carries a strip of sky through the beam.
  • PNU fixed telescope (historical): a replication of the same zenith-pointing design at another Saudi university.
  • PSU moving telescope, DISH222 (active): a steerable 5 m dish with a software-defined radio receiver. It can point at chosen positions, track them and map regions of sky by raster scanning.

A fixed telescope cannot choose where to look. That limit led to a steerable dish.

The historical data

The PSU fixed-telescope archive is valuable because several generations of the same observations survive.

  • Averaged spectra as TXT files: frequency against relative amplitude, with the receiver response already corrected. About one spectrum every two minutes.
  • MATLAB datasets of roughly 1000 spectra each, about 33 hours of observing.
  • A later MATLAB generation that includes the historical correction from solar time to sidereal time and Right Ascension.
  • A station-wide MATLAB structure combining the corrected PSU observations.

These generations let us do more than display old results. We can reconstruct and verify the whole processing chain.

From recorded spectra to scientific data

The historical records are always preserved unchanged. Modern products are generated separately and linked back through provenance.

  1. Time and sky position. Reconstruct UTC, local sidereal time and Right Ascension for the fixed declination of the zenith drift scan, and compare with the historical correction.
  2. Frequency and velocity. Keep the measured frequency spectrum and derive Doppler velocity in topocentric, barycentric, heliocentric and LSR frames.
  3. Intensity and temperature. Keep the relative intensity, and investigate whether the historical solar and other calibrations support a defensible temperature scale with stated uncertainty.
  4. Scientific archiving. Produce standardized FITS products with full metadata, processing history, calibration state and provenance, plus CSV, JSON and graphics where useful.
  5. Validation. Compare the results with professional H I surveys and published historical results, always separating what was measured from what the modern pipeline derived.

The end goal is for any historical observation to be viewable in steps of increasing meaning:

  1. Recorded spectrumMeasured
  2. Sky position (RA)Derived
  3. Doppler velocityDerived
  4. Calibrated temperatureDerived
  5. ComparisonInterpretation

The scientific objective

The aim is not just to modernize old file formats. It is to use the original observations to create a new, reproducible scientific dataset whose relationship to the historical measurements is completely documented, and eventually to publish standardized archive products.

Once the chain is understood on the simpler fixed telescope, the same principles carry over to DISH222, a richer system with targeted pointing, tracking and raster mapping.

How students take part

  • Reproduce the historical processing and verify it with modern astronomical tools.
  • Improve the spectral and calibration treatment.
  • Compare the results with reference surveys.
  • Work with real DISH222 observations, and help produce publishable data products.

New to the topic? The 52-slide interactive lecture teaches the principles behind the 21-cm line, the telescope, calibration and velocities. Published work is listed on the publications page.
