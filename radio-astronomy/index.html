Radio Astronomy

Observing the Milky Way through the H I 21-cm line

We study neutral hydrogen in the Milky Way. Historical fixed telescopes at PSU and PNU laid the foundation. The active steerable PSU telescope, DISH222, now maps the sky. Students can explore the data and take part in the research.

Explore the data Research

What we observe

Cold neutral hydrogen gas emits a faint radio line at 1420.40575 MHz. Its wavelength is about 21 cm.

The line passes through the dust that hides much of the Galaxy from optical telescopes. Its Doppler shift tells us how fast the gas moves towards or away from us.

  • The line shows where hydrogen gas is.
  • Its frequency shift gives the gas velocity.
  • Velocities along a line of sight trace the Galaxy's spiral structure and rotation.

The three telescopes

Today the portal focuses on three telescopes.

  • PSU fixed telescope: historical, zenith-pointing.
  • PNU fixed telescope: historical, zenith-pointing.
  • PSU moving telescope, DISH222: active. A 5 m dish on an azimuth/elevation mount with a software-defined radio receiver.

The fixed telescopes point straight up. The Earth's rotation carries the sky through the beam. DISH222 steers to chosen sky positions.

What data exist

Historical fixed telescopes recorded averaged spectra:

  • individual TXT spectra;
  • MATLAB (MAT) observation bundles;
  • bundles with historical RA/LST correction.

These files are not yet in the portal. Their archive structure is laid out and marked awaiting ingestion.

The moving telescope records averaged power spectra, not raw radio samples:

  • NPZ spectral observations and scan manifests;
  • spectral cubes, maps and other derived products;
  • every dataset tagged with a processing level (L0 to L8) and a calibration scale.

Both are described in the Data Library, which also holds the scientific archives.

From data to science

A recording becomes science through three transformations. Each has assumptions.

  • Time to sky position. Solar time becomes sidereal time, then Right Ascension.
  • Frequency to velocity. The Doppler shift becomes a velocity, in a stated reference frame.
  • Intensity to temperature. A relative signal is calibrated to kelvin.

These are taught step by step in Data to Science.

The active telescope today

DISH222 is the project's research telescope. It points to chosen positions, scans regions of sky and records spectra.

Its first large product is the 26 Aug 2026 outer-Galaxy raster shown above: 313 positions and 776 averaged spectra. It is the recommended starting dataset.

Where the project stands

A research project is never finished. This is an honest summary as of late September 2026, mostly about the DISH222 data.

Demonstrated

  • Automated raster scanning of the Galactic plane: the 26 Aug 2026 session visited 313 sky positions and recorded 776 averaged spectra.
  • H I brightness and velocity maps of the outer Galaxy, first on a relative (dB) scale.
  • Pointing improved from several degrees of error to about 1° after a Sun-based pointing calibration.
  • A kelvin temperature scale that went through five documented stages; the current one (C4) reproduces the LAB survey at eight reference positions with about 8.5 K scatter.

Still open

  • No complete observational product has yet been independently reproduced end-to-end, from native spectra to a final science product (nothing at level L8). Individual calibration calculations, such as the C4 eight-point fit, have been checked independently.
  • The current calibration (C4) has not been applied to any archived map; existing products stay on their original, historical scales.
  • The receiver noise temperature (about 860 K) limits sensitivity, and a small baseline pedestal is corrected only approximately.
  • Native spectra behind the September products and the all-sky composite are not yet in the research archive.

How to catch up quickly

  1. Understand the fixed telescopes and what they measured.
  2. Look at a historical spectrum (the PSU fixed archive is awaiting ingestion).
  3. Understand the RA / sidereal-time correction.
  4. Learn frequency to velocity and calibration.
  5. Explore DISH222 data in the Data Library.
  6. Choose a research question.

The 52-slide interactive lecture teaches the principles behind these steps.

Reading the portal:

  • C4 is the current calibration scale. Older products keep the scale they were made with.
  • Values marked inferred come from analysis, not a direct statement in a source.
  • Unknown details are listed as open questions rather than guessed.
